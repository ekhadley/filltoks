What the attention knockouts do

DeepSeek V4 Flash answers two-step arithmetic questions more often when the prompt carries a line of filler dots between the question and Answer: (0.47 → 0.62 mean probability of the right answer with 100 dots). The knockout experiment asks whether the dots need to see each other for that, or whether each dot helps on its own. It runs the same prompt with the attention mask edited so that chosen positions cannot attend to chosen other positions, at every layer, and reads the probability of the right answer at the last position.

The prompt

System prompt and 10 worked examples (about 1,850 tokens, computed once and cached), then the item: five variable definitions, the question, the label Filler:, k dots, Answer:, and the position where the answer is read. Only the item part is edited; the toy below shortens it to 24 dots and one-token definitions.

What a position can attend to in this model

Every layer has two branches. The exact branch sees the last 128 tokens one by one. The long-range branch sees pooled entries: in half the layers one entry per 4 tokens (each entry pools its own 4-token window and the one before, and an indexer keeps the 512 most relevant entries), in the other half one entry per 128 tokens. A knockout therefore has to block a key in the exact window and remove every pooled entry that covers it, otherwise the blocked token leaks through its entry. Removing an entry also hides the unblocked tokens it covers; with 100 dots that costs a few question tokens for about half the items, which the alone_keep_hca variant restores (same result).

The conditions as masks

Rows are query positions, columns are key positions, both in prompt order. Grey cells are visible, black cells are later tokens (never visible), orange cells are knocked out. A dot always sees itself and everything before the filler (the rows stay grey left of the dots), and the Answer: tokens and the answer position see every dot unless stated. Hover a cell for the two tokens.

  • full: nothing blocked, the ordinary prompt.
  • alone: a dot sees no other dot, so each dot is an independent computation and only the answer position can combine them.
  • alone, label hidden: as alone, and the dots cannot see the Filler: label either.
  • blocks of n (4, 8, 25 in the real run): a dot sees only the dots of its own block of n; compared with a prompt that has just n dots.
  • sliding 8: a dot sees the 8 dots before it; information can still travel farther by hopping from dot to dot, one hop per layer.
  • halves: the second half of the dots cannot see the first half.
  • answer blind: the dots see everything as usual, but Answer: and the answer position cannot see any dot.

Dots that cannot see each other are worse than no filler at all, and blocks of n dots are worse than n dots alone, so the dots are not independent attempts: the gain needs the dots to read each other, and a chain of short hops (sliding 8) recovers most of it. Blinding the answer to the dots removes the gain and more, so the answer position reads the dots directly rather than through the question tokens.
